Property 7 — Independence from the Measurement System The O-Ta laws are independent of the measurement system used to describe a quantity. If the measurement unit is changed, the numerical value representing the quantity changes, but the governing O-Ta laws remain unchanged. For example, a length may be represented using: millimeters (mm) centimeters (cm) meters (m) kilometers (km) and so on. Changing the unit changes only the numerical representation of the quantity. The underlying O-Ta laws do not change. Changing the measurement unit changes the numerical value, not the governing law. Therefore, the O-Ta system remains mathematically consistent regardless of the valid measurement unit used to represent its quantities. Property 8 — Geometric Conditions of the Object O-Ta formulas that depend on geometric quantities are applied according to the geometric structure of the object being considered. Depending on the formula, the relevant geometric quantities may include: diameter radius circumference area length width or other equivalent geometric quantities defined within the O-Ta model. These quantities describe the geometric properties that are relevant to a particular O-Ta calculation. Irregular Objects Objects do not necessarily have regular geometric shapes. Examples include: naturally formed rocks irregular pieces of soil deformed masses other naturally irregular objects. For such objects, certain geometric quantities may not have a unique or directly measurable value. For example, an irregular rock may not have a single well-defined diameter or radius. In such cases, a geometric O-Ta formula that specifically requires such a quantity cannot be directly evaluated using that undefined parameter. However, this does not mean that the object is outside the O-Ta system. The object remains subject to the governing O-Ta laws. Its irregular shape does not create an exception to those laws. The limitation applies only to the specific geometric representation required by a particular formula. Core Principle Irregular geometry does not exempt an object from the O-Ta laws. It only limits the direct application of geometric formulas whose required geometric quantities cannot be uniquely defined. Thus: Irregular object → certain geometric quantities may be undefined → the corresponding geometric formula cannot be directly evaluated → the O-Ta laws themselves remain unchanged and continue to apply. Relationship to the O-Ta System Property 8 therefore concerns the geometric applicability of individual formulas, not the validity of the O-Ta system itself. An object may have an irregular shape and still remain completely governed by O-Ta. The O-Ta laws do not require every object to have a simple or ideal geometric form. Only the formulas that explicitly depend on a particular geometric quantity require that quantity to be defined. No reduction of O-Ta's governing role occurs because of geometric irregularity. Summary Property 7 — Independence from the Measurement System Changing the measurement unit changes the numerical representation of a quantity, but does not change the O-Ta laws. Property 8 — Geometric Conditions of the Object Geometric O-Ta formulas depend on the geometric quantities required by those formulas. Irregular objects may lack uniquely defined geometric quantities, preventing direct evaluation of a particular formula. Nevertheless, the objects remain fully within the O-Ta system and remain subject to its governing laws. Warning: Content requires verification.

# O-Ta-Ko Simplified Model The **O-Ta-Ko Simplified Model** is a simplified geometric representation of the original O-Ta-Ko structure. The purpose of this model is to make the structure easier to construct, visualize, measure, and study using basic geometric methods. The simplified model is intended as a **supporting model** and does not replace the original O-Ta-Ko structure. ## 1. Basic Structure The simplified model consists of two main parts: - **Chum** — represented by a square block. - **Bình** — represented by a hollow circular cylindrical structure located in the central part of the Chum. This simplified representation replaces part of the more complex geometry of the original O-Ta-Ko structure with basic geometric shapes. ## 2. Standard Geometric Formulas The simplified model can be examined using standard formulas from elementary geometry. For example, the area of a square with side length `a` is: $$ S = a^2 $$ The circumference of a circle with radius `r` is: $$ C = 2\pi r $$ The area of a circle with radius `r` is: $$ S = \pi r^2 $$ The volume of a cylinder with radius `r` and height `h` is: $$ V = \pi r^2 h $$ These are **standard geometric formulas**. They are used only as examples for calculations involving the simplified model and are not newly derived formulas of O-Ta-Ko. ## 3. Why Use a Simplified Model? The simplified model makes the structure easier to work with because basic geometric shapes can be constructed and measured more easily. It can be useful for: - simple geometric calculations; - physical model construction; - visualization; - measurements and experiments; - explaining the general structure of O-Ta-Ko. The simplified model therefore provides a practical way to study some geometric aspects of O-Ta-Ko without reproducing the full complexity of the original structure. ## 4. Main Limitation The main limitation of the simplified model is its treatment of the connection between **Chum** and **Bình**. In the original O-Ta-Ko structure, this connection contains a **smooth and continuous curved transition**. The simplified model replaces this curved transition with simpler geometric forms. Because of this simplification, the connection may contain a **sharp transition, such as a 90° corner**, instead of the smooth curvature of the original structure. This means that the simplified model is easier to construct and calculate, but it does not completely preserve the geometric continuity of the original O-Ta-Ko structure. ## 5. Original Model vs. Simplified Model | Feature | Original O-Ta-Ko Model | Simplified Model | |---|---|---| | Geometry | More complex curved structure | Basic geometric shapes | | Connection | Smooth and continuous | Simplified transition | | Calculation | More difficult | Easier | | Construction | More difficult | Easier | | Measurement | More complex | Easier | | Role | Primary model | Supporting model | ## Conclusion The **O-Ta-Ko Simplified Model** provides a simpler geometric representation of the original O-Ta-Ko structure. It allows standard elementary-geometry formulas to be used for basic calculations and makes physical construction, measurement, and visualization easier. However, the simplification also removes part of the smooth curved transition present in the original structure. Therefore, the simplified model should be considered a **supporting model for O-Ta-Ko**, while the **original O-Ta-Ko structure remains the primary model**.

# O-Ta-Ko Simplified Model

The **O-Ta-Ko Simplified Model** is a simplified geometric representation of the original O-Ta-Ko structure.

The purpose of this model is to make the structure easier to construct, visualize, measure, and study using basic geometric methods.

The simplified model is intended as a **supporting model** and does not replace the original O-Ta-Ko structure.

## 1. Basic Structure

The simplified model consists of two main parts:

- **Chum** — represented by a square block.
- **Bình** — represented by a hollow circular cylindrical structure located in the central part of the Chum.

This simplified representation replaces part of the more complex geometry of the original O-Ta-Ko structure with basic geometric shapes.

## 2. Standard Geometric Formulas

The simplified model can be examined using standard formulas from elementary geometry.

For example, the area of a square with side length `a` is:

$$
S = a^2
$$

The circumference of a circle with radius `r` is:

$$
C = 2\pi r
$$

The area of a circle with radius `r` is:

$$
S = \pi r^2
$$

The volume of a cylinder with radius `r` and height `h` is:

$$
V = \pi r^2 h
$$

These are **standard geometric formulas**. They are used only as examples for calculations involving the simplified model and are not newly derived formulas of O-Ta-Ko.

## 3. Why Use a Simplified Model?

The simplified model makes the structure easier to work with because basic geometric shapes can be constructed and measured more easily.

It can be useful for:

- simple geometric calculations;
- physical model construction;
- visualization;
- measurements and experiments;
- explaining the general structure of O-Ta-Ko.

The simplified model therefore provides a practical way to study some geometric aspects of O-Ta-Ko without reproducing the full complexity of the original structure.

## 4. Main Limitation

The main limitation of the simplified model is its treatment of the connection between **Chum** and **Bình**.

In the original O-Ta-Ko structure, this connection contains a **smooth and continuous curved transition**.

The simplified model replaces this curved transition with simpler geometric forms. Because of this simplification, the connection may contain a **sharp transition, such as a 90° corner**, instead of the smooth curvature of the original structure.

This means that the simplified model is easier to construct and calculate, but it does not completely preserve the geometric continuity of the original O-Ta-Ko structure.

## 5. Original Model vs. Simplified Model

| Feature | Original O-Ta-Ko Model | Simplified Model |
|---|---|---|
| Geometry | More complex curved structure | Basic geometric shapes |
| Connection | Smooth and continuous | Simplified transition |
| Calculation | More difficult | Easier |
| Construction | More difficult | Easier |
| Measurement | More complex | Easier |
| Role | Primary model | Supporting model |

## Conclusion

The **O-Ta-Ko Simplified Model** provides a simpler geometric representation of the original O-Ta-Ko structure.

It allows standard elementary-geometry formulas to be used for basic calculations and makes physical construction, measurement, and visualization easier.

However, the simplification also removes part of the smooth curved transition present in the original structure.

Therefore, the simplified model should be considered a **supporting model for O-Ta-Ko**, while the **original O-Ta-Ko structure remains the primary model**.

# O-Ta Theory — Property 6: Infinite Nesting

If the O-Ta nesting process continues indefinitely, the laws governing
the O-Ta system remain unchanged.

Only the representative value C changes according to the nesting level.

Therefore, infinite nesting does not create a new governing law at every
level. The O-Ta principle continues to operate regardless of the depth
of nesting.

---

## 2. Representative-Value Sequence

For successive O-Ta levels, the representative values follow a strictly
decreasing order:

a₁ > a₂ > a₃ > ...

Equivalently:

aₙ₊₁ < aₙ

This describes the change in the representative value as the nesting
process continues.

---

## 3. Two-Level O-Ta Structure

Consider two O-Ta structures:

B¹ and B².

B² is nested inside B¹.

When B² is nested into B¹, the bottom of B² does not completely reach
the corresponding position of B¹.

Instead, the bottom of B² is displaced by one interval relative to the
previous position.

---

## 4. Recursive Nesting

The displacement created by one O-Ta nesting becomes the stopping
position for the next O-Ta.

The process can therefore continue:

B¹ → B² → B³ → B⁴ → ...

The same O-Ta rule continues to be applied at each successive level.

---

## 5. Infinite Continuation

When the O-Ta nesting process continues without limit:

B¹ → B² → B³ → ...

the governing O-Ta laws remain unchanged.

The representative values continue to follow:

a₁ > a₂ > a₃ > ...

while C changes according to the nesting level.

---

## 6. Nesting-Offset Principle

At each nesting operation, the bottom of the next O-Ta is displaced by
one additional interval relative to the preceding O-Ta.

This displacement becomes the stopping position for the next nesting
operation.

The process is therefore repeated from one nesting level to the next.

---

## 7. Infinite O-Ta Nesting

Let the nesting continue indefinitely:

B¹ ⊃ B² ⊃ B³ ⊃ ... ⊃ Bⁿ ⊃ ...

The corresponding representative values satisfy:

a₁ > a₂ > a₃ > ... > aₙ > ...

The governing O-Ta law remains unchanged throughout the nesting process.

---

## 8. Summary

Property 6 establishes:

1. O-Ta nesting can continue indefinitely.
2. The governing O-Ta laws remain unchanged.
3. The representative value C changes according to the nesting level.
4. The representative values follow a strictly decreasing order:

   a₁ > a₂ > a₃ > ...

5. The bottom of the next O-Ta is displaced by one additional interval.
6. This displacement becomes the stopping position for the following
   O-Ta.
7. The nesting process can therefore continue recursively:

   B¹ → B² → B³ → ...

Property 7 — Independence from the Measurement System

The O-Ta laws are independent of the measurement system used to describe a quantity.

If the measurement unit is changed, the numerical value representing the quantity changes, but the governing O-Ta laws remain unchanged.

For example, a length may be represented using:

millimeters (mm)
centimeters (cm)
meters (m)
kilometers (km)
and so on.

Changing the unit changes only the numerical representation of the quantity.

The underlying O-Ta laws do not change.

Changing the measurement unit changes the numerical value, not the governing law.

Therefore, the O-Ta system remains mathematically consistent regardless of the valid measurement unit used to represent its quantities.

Property 8 — Geometric Conditions of the Object

O-Ta formulas that depend on geometric quantities are applied according to the geometric structure of the object being considered.

Depending on the formula, the relevant geometric quantities may include:

diameter
radius
circumference
area
length
width
or other equivalent geometric quantities defined within the O-Ta model.

These quantities describe the geometric properties that are relevant to a particular O-Ta calculation.

Irregular Objects

Objects do not necessarily have regular geometric shapes.

Examples include:

naturally formed rocks
irregular pieces of soil
deformed masses
other naturally irregular objects.

For such objects, certain geometric quantities may not have a unique or directly measurable value.

For example, an irregular rock may not have a single well-defined diameter or radius.

In such cases, a geometric O-Ta formula that specifically requires such a quantity cannot be directly evaluated using that undefined parameter.

However, this does not mean that the object is outside the O-Ta system.

The object remains subject to the governing O-Ta laws.

Its irregular shape does not create an exception to those laws.

The limitation applies only to the specific geometric representation required by a particular formula.

Core Principle

Irregular geometry does not exempt an object from the O-Ta laws. It only limits the direct application of geometric formulas whose required geometric quantities cannot be uniquely defined.

Thus:

Irregular object → certain geometric quantities may be undefined → the corresponding geometric formula cannot be directly evaluated → the O-Ta laws themselves remain unchanged and continue to apply.

Relationship to the O-Ta System

Property 8 therefore concerns the geometric applicability of individual formulas, not the validity of the O-Ta system itself.

An object may have an irregular shape and still remain completely governed by O-Ta.

The O-Ta laws do not require every object to have a simple or ideal geometric form.

Only the formulas that explicitly depend on a particular geometric quantity require that quantity to be defined.

No reduction of O-Ta's governing role occurs because of geometric irregularity.

Summary

Property 7 — Independence from the Measurement System

Changing the measurement unit changes the numerical representation of a quantity, but does not change the O-Ta laws.

Property 8 — Geometric Conditions of the Object

Geometric O-Ta formulas depend on the geometric quantities required by those formulas. Irregular objects may lack uniquely defined geometric quantities, preventing direct evaluation of a particular formula. Nevertheless, the objects remain fully within the O-Ta system and remain subject to its governing laws.
